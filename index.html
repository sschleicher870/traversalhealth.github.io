Who we are

Cancer is currently the leading cause of death in U.S. prisons. Cancer care in this setting is very difficult, predominantly driven by challenges associated with security-escorted, off-site visits for every oncology appointment and treatment. These barriers can delay treatment, strain correctional health systems and drive up costs for everyone involved.

That's why we - two practicing medical oncologists passionate about access to care for underserved patients - founded Traversal Health.

Sources: Manz et al., JAMA Network Open, 2025; U.S. Bureau of Justice Statistics. See references 1, 2.

Our mission

Timely, high-quality, compassionate cancer care inside the walls of the prison, meeting patients and care teams where they are and reducing challenges for everyone involved.

Our story

Founded by two oncologists committed to bringing the best cancer care to every patient, wherever they are.

Chris Manz, MD, MSHP

Co-Founder & Medical Oncologist

As an oncologist and researcher at Dana-Farber and Harvard Medical School, Chris set out to learn how cancer care in prison could be improved. He led studies of more than 2 million cancer cases that show how incarceration affects survival,3 and interviewed correctional medical directors, clinicians, and oncologists across 16 prison systems1,5 to learn how to improve cancer care for incarcerated patients. He left Harvard to implement these solutions with Traversal.

Stephen Schleicher, MD, FASCO

Co-Founder & Medical Oncologist

Inspired by his mother's work as the first chaplain at the Tennessee Prison for Women, Stephen built his career around patients who are too often overlooked. After residency at Harvard and fellowship at Memorial Sloan Kettering, he became Chief Medical Officer of Tennessee Oncology. He also serves as chair-elect of ASCO's Quality Certification Steering Group.

How care is delivered changes the outcome. In a study of 106,000 people in California prisons, patients who had not been screened in the three months before diagnosis were twice as likely to be diagnosed at stage IV (21% vs. 10%). Recently screened patients were also 37% less likely to die.4

One facility's daily transport capacity

A real-world example from Dr. Manz's national study: at one prison, custody staffing allowed only four escorted trips a day, and three cancer patients on daily radiation needed three of them. That left one trip for every other outside appointment.1

For corrections partners

A world-class oncology clinic, inside the walls.

Lower security risk

Every trip outside the prison is a security risk: regular recurring clinic visits, public roads, hospital waiting rooms, and officers off post. Keeping care inside lowers these risks.

Fewer transports

Based on common cancers and treatment regimens, we estimate that 30 patients on active treatment delivered outside of prison have around 400 off-site visits each year. We move consults and treatment on-site, so trips out are reserved for surgery, radiation, and advanced imaging.

Better outcomes

Every month of delay in cancer treatment can raise the risk of death by around 10%.6 Our oncologists help guide workups from first suspicion of malignancy, and our supportive care helps patients tolerate treatment, with fewer ER visits and hospitalizations.
